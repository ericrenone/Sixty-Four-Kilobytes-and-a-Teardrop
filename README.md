# Sixty-Four Kilobytes and a Teardrop

*How Dragon Quest, Zelda, and three designers who never trained for their medium turned hard constraints into icons that have outlasted everything richer.*

### At a glance

- **Two games, ten days apart.** On February 11, 1986, *Weekly Shōnen Jump* explained to its young readers what a role-playing game was. *The Legend of Zelda* shipped ten days later (February 21), and *Dragon Quest* followed fifteen weeks after the feature (May 27). [39, 46, 67]
- **The illustrator did not know what an RPG was.** Akira Toriyama accepted the job because his editor ordered it. Horii was told "Toriyama wants to work on this"; Toriyama later said, "I never said that." [4, 16]
- **Sixty-four kilobytes wrote the design.** The memory ceiling produced the single hero, the tanka-form password, the 7-XP first level-up, and the locked throne room. [1, 4]
- **The Slime inverts its brief.** Horii asked for a shapeless, face-suffocating goo; Toriyama returned a teardrop with two dots and a smile, which has needed no redesign since. [16, 53]
- **Kurita did not invent emoji, and Toriyama did not invent the poop emoji.** J-Phone shipped 90 glyphs, pile of poo included, on November 1, 1997. *Dr. Slump* primed the culture; no source ties the glyph to Toriyama. [71, 75]
- **Jobs's calligraphy teacher was Robert Palladino, not Lloyd Reynolds; Susan Kare drew the Mac fonts.** Jobs's documented typographic acts are the "world class cities" renaming, the proportional-type push, and the PostScript deal. [89, 96, 114]
- **Method, not genius.** An adjacent discipline supplied the standard, an external judge supplied a number, and a resource ceiling kept only the identity-bearing marks.

## 1. Three designers who did not train for their medium

Steve Jobs was born in San Francisco on February 24, 1955, and adopted by machinist Paul Jobs and bookkeeper Clara Jobs. [101] Forty days later, on April 5, 1955, Akira Toriyama was born in Kiyosu, Aichi, where he would live and work for the rest of his life. [133] Shigeru Miyamoto, born November 16, 1952 in Sonobe, Kyoto, was the elder of the three by two and a half years. [68] All grew up inside the postwar consumer-media boom, shaped by imports: Toriyama credits Disney's *One Hundred and One Dalmatians* (1961), Miyamoto cites manga structure and Western genre television, and Jobs grew up on Hewlett-Packard parts and, from 1970, a Sōtō Zen center in Los Altos. [68, 113, 133]

None trained for the medium that made him. Jobs enrolled at Reed College in fall 1972, was officially a student for one semester, then stayed about eighteen months auditing classes, calligraphy among them. [87, 88] Toriyama attended a high school design course ("I had no intention of becoming a cartoonist to begin with; I wanted to do graphic design"), then spent about three years at a Nagoya design firm producing "almost all flyers." [122] Miyamoto wanted to draw manga and earned an industrial-design degree at Kanazawa in the early 1970s; the exact year (often 1975, sometimes 1977) is unconfirmed. Nintendo hired him in 1977 on the strength of his toy creations. [68]

The pattern repeats. An adjacent discipline supplied the standard (letterform spacing, poster legibility, industrial form). A powerful older figure supplied the constraint (Mike Markkula's 1977 marketing doctrine, Kazuhiko Torishima's rejected pages, Hiroshi Yamauchi's hardware). And the defining product arrived inside one two-year window: the Macintosh on January 24, 1984; *Dragon Ball* in 1984; *Super Mario Bros.* on September 13, 1985; and then the spring of 1986. [60, 101, 133] In that spring, the two games that founded the two halves of the Japanese console canon shipped three months apart, and the illustrator of one had no idea what he had agreed to.

## 2. Dragon Quest origin story

### 2.1 Enix, the 1982 contest, and *Portopia*

*Dragon Quest*'s publisher began as a real-estate advertiser. Yasuhiro Fukushima founded Eidansha Boshu Service Center on September 22, 1975 to publish property tabloids; it became **Enix Corporation on August 30, 1982**, the name blending "Phoenix" and "ENIAC." [54] Its entry into software was a talent search, the 1982 Enix Game Hobby Program Contest (¥1 million prize), which produced Yuji Horii, Koichi Nakamura, and Kazuro Morita. [5, 54] Nakamura, then a high-school student, took the runner-up prize for programming with *Door Door*. [57] Torishima's summary is the best one-line account of the series' origin:

> "What was amazing about that contest was that 'a game designer who barely knew programming' and 'a genius programmer with no PC' appeared at the same time."
> — Kazuhiko Torishima, 2023 [1]

Horii, a Waseda literature graduate writing for newspapers and magazines, had a literary motive was literary: "I had wanted to be a manga artist, so I thought it'd be interesting to write a story using the interactivity of computer games." [1]

Before *Dragon Quest*, Horii shipped a tutorial for it. His 1983 PC adventure *The Portopia Serial Murder Case* came to the Famicom in 1985, the first Horii-Nakamura collaboration. The Famicom had no keyboard, so a selectable command menu replaced typed sentences; Horii's stated purpose was "I wanted users to get used to the idea of a game you play by reading text." [1, 4, 7, 56] Those commands carried over directly into *Dragon Quest*. [9]

### 2.2 *Wizardry*, *Ultima*, and who liked which

On a Shueisha-funded US trip, Torishima's group bought an Apple II-compatible and played *Ultima* and *Wizardry* together. His diagnosis: *Wizardry*'s world and combat were fun but its fields monotonous; *Ultima*'s fields were good but its combat was not. The plan was to "take the best of both." [1, 2] The date of Horii's first encounter with *Wizardry* is disputed: the Strong Museum says the 1983 AppleFest in San Francisco, TouchArcade says 1982, and Wikipedia says a Macworld Expo during the *Portopia* port (about 1985). [8, 46, 66]

> [!NOTE]
> **Correction to a common version.** Horii is routinely called the *Wizardry* devotee and Nakamura the *Ultima* fan. Nakamura says the reverse, twice: Horii was "really into *Ultima*" while he himself "was really into *Wizardry*." The accurate statement is that Nakamura and Chunsoft were the *Wizardry* camp, Horii and the *Jump* staff the *Ultima* camp, and Horii loved both. [4, 6, 7]

Nakamura's statement of intent is the simplest: *Dragon Quest* was "an easier, more accessible version of *Ultima* or *Wizardry*. We wanted to introduce RPGs to Japan, to kids, to everybody." [6]

### 2.3 Torishima's plan: Toriyama, exclusivity, no IP

Torishima was both Toriyama's editor and Horii's, and the collaboration was his construction: "I decided to add Toriyama to the project for the character designs." An illustrated character would catch the eye on a magazine page, and he wanted Toriyama working outside manga, since "working with different people broadens your range." [1, 2, 3]

The commercial structure was deliberately lopsided. Enix paid for development; "Shueisha would not own the intellectual property. That was something I intentionally did," because "my manager then didn't understand games." In exchange *Jump* paid no royalties and got exclusive coverage and the strategy guides, so that "the book profits flowed to Shueisha." Horii's verdict: "I really felt he was fighting his own company." [1, 2, 3]

How Toriyama was recruited is the most-quoted exchange in the series' history, and both sides agree it involved a white lie:

> "Apparently Toriyama was a big fan of *Portopia*. ... [Torishima told me] 'Toriyama wants to work on this.' ... Later Toriyama said, 'I never said that. Torishima ordered me to do it.'"
> — Yuji Horii, *Game Maestro* vol. 1 (2000) [4]

By 2018 Horii thought Toriyama "never really had an interest," and in 2023 he said simply, "Torishima was clever about this." Toriyama's own printed account is that he did not quite understand the concept but accepted because of Torishima's insistence and some side money. [1, 9, 16]

### 2.4 Sugiyama's postcard

Koichi Sugiyama, a classically trained composer (born April 11, 1931) who wrote commercial music, entered Enix through a questionnaire. In August 1985 he filled out the player-survey postcard from *Morita's Shogi* as a joke; producer Chida, surprised to find a celebrity among the responses, hired him for *Wingman 2* and then *Dragon Quest*. [42, 58]

The "five-minute overture" is self-reported: the claim originates with Sugiyama, whose own site reframes it as "5 minutes + 54 years," and no independent witness confirms it. [45, 46]

### 2.5 Development and everything 64 KB forced

Horii and Nakamura began development in November 1985, choosing the Famicom because PCs were too expensive for children and because players would not lose money on a game over and could resume where they left off. [1, 39, 46] Horii's scenario ran "about 50" manuscript pages (versus 16,000 for *Dragon Quest VII*). [4, 6] The often-repeated "five months of programming" has no source. What is documented is that *Dragon Quest II* planning began in April 1986, and that Toriyama wrote in *Jump* on May 13, 1986 that "*Dragon Quest* is dangerous! It got to the point where I couldn't get any work done, so my wife finally took it away." [16, 48]

The governing constraint was memory. "There was only 64 kilobytes in total," Horii said in 2023, music included. [1] What it forced:

| Constraint | Consequence | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| **Single hero, not a party** | Horii wanted several characters but had to use one; his 1987 "Making of DQII" called the first game's combat "tedious," motivating the party in *II* | [9, 46, 48] |
| **Katakana cut by over half** | "We had to get rid of more than half of the katakana characters"; all hiragana kept for the password | [1, 5] |
| **Level cap of 30** | Capacity was a factor | [1] |
| **Password as a tanka** | A numeric password would have run 40-50 characters; hiragana shortened it, and "we used a 5-7-5-7-7 tanka" | [4, 7] |
| **Locked throne room** | "If you allow players to go anywhere from the outset, they won't know where to go." Forced "Talk" and "Search" "teaches players the basic controls" | [4] |
| **First level-up at 7 XP** | "Easier for players to understand" | [4] |
| **Visible goal, scaled danger** | Charlock Castle visible from the start; monsters harder with distance from Tantegel; bridges as difficulty gates | [46] |
| **Gentle death** | Halved gold, no XP loss; "very gentle for the time" versus *Wizardry*'s corpse retrieval | [1] |

Horii's framing: RPGs were "quite niche back then, and oftentimes, people didn't quite know what to do in the games. We wanted to create a kind of linear rail that people can go on to experience the genre." [9]

### 2.6 The *Jump* feature, release, and slow start

*Famicom Shinken*, which Torishima launched with Horii over his superiors' fears of backlash, ran 1985-1988 (the Dragon Quest Wiki says 1989). [2, 40, 56] Torishima persuaded his superiors to cover an unreleased game, and the first *Dragon Quest* feature ran in *Jump* issue 11, **dated February 11, 1986**, with an explanation of what an RPG is, screenshots, and Toriyama art. Exclusive content continued until release. [39] Horii called the coverage very lucky: it taught readers "this is what an RPG is, and this is how fun it is." [1, 4]

*Dragon Quest* shipped on the Famicom on **May 27, 1986**. [46] Sales began slowly. Nakamura recalls a first run of about 0.6 million and eventual sales of **1.1 million**; Chris Kohler's *Power-Up*, via Wikipedia, gives about **1.5 million** in Japan and over 2 million worldwide. No source reconciles the two. [5, 6, 46] Torishima explains the caution: cartridge ROMs were paid for up front, so unsold stock was a total loss. [1] At the 1987 *Famitsu* awards (for 1986) the game won Game of the Year, Best Scenario, Best Character Design, Best Programmer, and Best RPG. [46]

## 3. Toriyama with no game experience

### 3.1 The brief

Toriyama designed the hero, the Dragonlord, the princess, and the monsters. He wrote no story or dialogue. [16] The process set in 1986 held for thirty-eight years: Horii conceived designs as notes and doodles, Toriyama redrew them, Horii approved. [46, 133] Horii wanted a cartoonist because the Famicom meant "we wouldn't really be able to achieve a truly realistic imagery or graphic style," and chibi-style art gave the characters humor. [9] Toriyama's own foreword to *Dragon Quest Illustrations* admits "I wasn't all that knowledgeable about fantasy worlds in the first place." [18]

### 3.2 The Slime in full

The Slime is the cleanest case study in reduction because the brief survives:

> "No fixed shape. A gelatinous, goopy monster. It attaches itself to people's faces, etc. and suffocates them."
> — Yuji Horii, concept sheet for the Slime [16]

Horii, "really hooked on *Wizardry*," took inspiration from one of its enemies, conceived "a pile of goo," doodled it, and passed it to Toriyama, who recalled receiving "the gloopy slime monster that basically looked like a puddle." [16, 18, 53] His first attempt was rejected. In Horii's 2023 account Toriyama first drew the slime in pixel art, and Horii said "that's not it," because "in pixel art there was none of Toriyama's flavor." What came back was a teardrop, which Horii considered "perfect." Naming followed drawing: "I looked at the pictures Toriyama drew and named them one by one." [1, 53]

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The design inverts every term of the brief.** "No fixed shape" became the most fixed shape in Japanese games; a face-hugger became a face; horror became cuteness. The teardrop point gives the silhouette an orientation that reads at sprite scale, and two dots with a curved mouth are the minimum grammar needed to register an expression.

The mascot status followed from persistence as much as form. Horii (2001) attributed its popularity to cuteness, ease of defeat, and constant presence while protagonists change; GamesRadar (2008) called it "training wheels" whose smile makes players hesitate (a critic's reading, not a creator's); GameSpy's William Cassidy noted that asked to draw "Slime," a Japanese person typically draws this onion shape. [44, 53]

The West initially rejected the style: "the people over there said 'this manga art won't do!'" The 1989 *Dragon Warrior* stripped his artwork, and only from *Dragon Warrior VII* (2001) did localizations retain it. [1, 126]

### 3.3 The 2016 NHK message

For the thirtieth anniversary, NHK aired a message from Toriyama (late December 2016). He said he was easily persuaded and did not know what an RPG was; that he never imagined thirty years and "would have declined had he known"; that many early designs were rejected for not fitting series guidelines; and that design is "fun, but also rather difficult" because each game uses roughly the same fantasy period, so "we have to use every trick in the book." [20] Lines about being "old now" or "running out of monsters" do not appear in the translation. Asked in 2003 whether the shared word "Dragon" was deliberate: "It's just a coincidence." [17]

### 3.4 Why flyer training survives 16×16 pixels

Toriyama's one stated principle of game design is a rule about scale:

> "You just have to give them a design with a feature that you can tell 'it's this character' even when they're small."
> — Akira Toriyama, *Dragon Ball Daizenshuu 5* (1995) [16]

It is a graphic designer's rule. Three years of posters and flyers means making one image and a few words read at distance and at a glance; Torishima picked him up because "You were good at hand-lettering." [122, 123] With no money for screentone, he mastered solid black-and-white work. [133] French critic Jean-Paul Jennequin supplied the structural test: "Akira Toriyama's drawings do not lose anything when reversed," the symmetry check a logo designer runs. [124] A closed contour with no interior dependency survives a flipped page, a 16×16 sprite, a cel, and a plush toy for the same reason. Horii's tribute on Toriyama's death credited exactly this: "elevating the characters and monsters beyond hardware limitations." [41]

## 4. Zelda and the Famicom moment

### 4.1 The market

The Family Computer launched on July 15, 1983 at ¥14,800, sold 500,000 in two months, and was Japan's best-selling console by the end of 1984. [70] By 1986 console games "outsold computer games significantly." [65] Both 1986 games assumed a child encountering the form for the first time. Miyamoto: "a lot of people would be playing an adventure game of that sort for the first time." Horii: "people didn't quite know what to do in the games." [9, 60]

### 4.2 *Zelda*: fall 1984 to February 21, 1986

*The Legend of Zelda* shipped in Japan on **February 21, 1986** as the launch title of the Famicom Disk System, reaching North America on August 22, 1987. [67] Development began in fall 1984 on the same team that was finishing *Super Mario Bros.* Miyamoto and Takashi Tezuka directed, Toshihiko Nakago programmed, and Koji Kondo composed. Miyamoto: "we decided to finish up *Super Mario Bros.* first." [60, 67] The manual backstory was written by Keiji Terui, a screenwriter on the *Dr. Slump* and *Dragon Ball* anime, the one documented crossover between Toriyama's world and *Zelda*. [67]

Kondo's title theme was, for most of development, Ravel's *Boléro*:

> "For quite a while, it just played Ravel's *Boléro*. It really matched the opening crawl! ... immediately before finishing *The Legend of Zelda*, we learned it was still under copyright ... So I pulled an all-nighter to compose the opening song."
> — Koji Kondo, 2016 [60]

Hardware shaped the game as precisely as 64 KB shaped *Dragon Quest*. The Disk System launched the same day at ¥15,000; its disks held 112 KB (56 per side; the *Zelda* article says 128 KB nominal), and the rewritable disk replaced passwords with saves. [67, 69] Because the Famicom supported only **256 tiles**, all text used katakana only. [67] The game sold 1.69 million on the Disk System and **6.51 million worldwide**. [67] The save system was a hardware consequence that the cartridge-bound *Dragon Quest*, three months later, could not share, which is why one game saves and the other recites a tanka.

### 4.3 Miyamoto: caves, manga, and the 16×16 Mario

Miyamoto's two competences, character legibility under constraint and spatial thinking, come from two unfinished vocations. As a boy in Sonobe he explored fields, woods, and caves, entering one only after days of hesitation; a maze of sliding *fusuma* doors at home became the dungeons. [60, 67, 68]

Mario is the purest case in this report of form dictated by a pixel budget:

> "The original Mario was a 16 X 16 pixilated image. ... if you draw a nose then a moustache, you don't really know if it's a mouth or a moustache, and it saves pixels. ... because you can't fully draw hair, by making him wear a hat, you can reduce the hair to only a couple of pixels."
> — Shigeru Miyamoto, Iwata Asks [61]

With only three animation frames, "in order to make that movement easier to see, I thought it would be best to make his arms and his body different colors," hence the overalls. Satoru Iwata summarized: "the entire design was a case of form being dictated by function." [61]

### 4.4 Japanese RPG precursors

Both games arrived into a small, PC-bound tradition. *The Black Onyx* (1984) "did more to champion video game RPGs in Japan than perhaps any other game," and *Hydlide*'s Famicom port sold a million. [65, 66] Szczepaniak divides the history into "experimental RPGs before *Dragon Quest*, and everything which came after." [65]

## 5. Zelda versus Dragon Quest

Both games teach by spatial gating rather than instruction, and they are near-perfect inversions of each other.

| Dimension | *The Legend of Zelda* | *Dragon Quest* |
|---|---|---|
| Release (Japan) | Feb 21, 1986, Disk System [67] | May 27, 1986, cartridge [46] |
| Self-description | "A real-time adventure game" [67] | "Extremely basic, orthodox role-playing game" [4] |
| First screen | Empty; one cave; one old man: "It's dangerous to go alone! Take this." [64] | King's chamber; exit locked until "Talk" and "Search" are used [4, 46] |
| Text on first screen | One line | Entirely mandatory text |
| Who holds the leash | The player, from screen one | The designer, until the controls are learned |
| Help philosophy | Sealed map: "everyone breaks the seal" [60] | Visible castle, 7-XP level-up, difficulty by distance [4, 46] |
| Social model | Players "calling each other at night to exchange information" [60] | Guaranteed progress: "if you put in the work and get stronger you can manage to progress" [4] |
| Persistence | Disk saves | Hiragana tanka password |
| Western reception | Widely praised; 6.51M sold [67] | *Nintendo Power* about 3/5; ~2M sold [46] |

> "You should only use the map and strategic tips as a last resort. ... But everyone breaks the seal."
> — Shigeru Miyamoto, on the sealed map [60]

The two openings share one assumption: the lesson should be an environment, not a pop-up. The opening was a deliberate restoration; early designs had Link start with the sword, and Miyamoto added the old man to encourage players to share secrets. [67]

A 1989 published conversation between Miyamoto and Horii shows the gap. Miyamoto said menu-driven adventures make it "hard for players to feel they solved puzzles themselves," that he dislikes taking control from players, and that *Adventure of Link*'s cutscenes "make you watch rather than act." His teams aim for about a 20 percent difficulty reduction from the designers' first instinct, and build level 2 before level 1. Horii said players identify with the avatar, so "only the protagonist's lines will stick in the players' heads"; novel-style storytelling makes a game "drag"; and "no matter what happens, you have to be allowed to undo it." [62] The two were cordial: Miyamoto was "amazed by how much data you packed into *Portopia*." [62, 63] Miyamoto values the player's sense of authorship of a solution; Horii values guaranteed progress for effort. The two games are two answers to the same child.

**Awards and reception.** *Famitsu*'s 1986 awards split the year: *Dragon Quest* took Game of the Year and Best RPG, *Zelda* third place and Best BGM. [46, 67] The gap in Western reception tracks the gap in sales.

**Final Fantasy.** *Dragon Quest*'s most consequential follower was *Final Fantasy* (December 18, 1987). Sakaguchi, in February 2025:

> "I had to abandon developing RPGs due to the limited storage capacity of the ROM cartridge. ... *Dragon Quest* overcame these barriers with creative ideas. ... I scolded myself for thinking that creating an RPG was not possible on Famicom."
> — Hironobu Sakaguchi [36]

Critics' consensus is that *Dragon Quest* established the JRPG not by inventing its parts but by combining *Wizardry*'s battles, *Ultima*'s exploration, and *Portopia*'s menus into an accessible whole. [59, 66]

> [!NOTE]
> **An absence.** No documented statement by Akira Toriyama about Nintendo, Miyamoto, or *The Legend of Zelda* exists in any published source. His documented relationship to games was as an assigned illustrator ("Torishima ordered me to do it") and an enthusiastic player, not as a participant in the design conversation of 1986. [4, 62]

At the June 2018 shareholders meeting Miyake had said the most important thing was "having Horii-san, Sugiyama-sensei, Akira Toriyama-san work together." [21]

### 6.2 Totals, the merger, and the spin-offs

Square Enix's cumulative figure counts physical shipments plus digital sales: over 85 million as of March 2022, 95 million as of June 2025, over 97 million as of February 2026, and, announced on **September 16, 2026** for the fortieth anniversary, more than **100 million**. [30, 31, 47] The Enix-Square merger took effect April 1, 2003, with Enix the surviving company. [55]

The Slime and the system traveled without the hero. *Torneko no Daibōken* (1993) was the first Mystery Dungeon game; *Dragon Quest Monsters* (1998) carried Toriyama designs; the Slime got its own sub-series. [41, 47] *Dragon Quest Walk* (COLOPL, September 12, 2019) passed 5 million downloads in its first week and earned an estimated **$201 million** in 2019. [52] Secondary summaries credit influence on *Pokémon*, *Digimon*, and *Final Fantasy*, but Satoshi Tajiri's own 1999 account of *Pokémon*'s origins cites Ultraman's capsule monsters, insect collecting, and the link cable, not *Dragon Quest*, so that link is attribution rather than testimony. [37, 47]

### 6.3 February 10, 1988: launch day and the law that never existed

*Dragon Quest III* launched on **Thursday, February 10, 1988**. Over a million copies sold on the first day, 3 million in the first week. [47, 49] Japanese TV reported **392 truant children and nine thefts** nationwide; *Compute!* reported "nearly 300 arrests for truancy"; Wikipedia gives "over 300." None is a primary police figure. The Ministry of Education sent a fax urging stores to "avoid weekday launches." [13, 47]

> [!WARNING]
> **There was no "Dragon Quest law."** Retronauts found no hearings, ultimatums, or statute in archival footage; Time Extension calls the law "completely false." Executive producer Yuu Miyake told Game Informer that after the police said "You guys need to do something about this," Enix began selling only on Saturdays, and told IGN the company arranged a Saturday release with Nintendo "as a special exception." One Wikipedia sentence asserts Diet hearings on muggings, which the archival footage does not support. [12, 13, 14, 49]

### 6.4 *Dragon Warrior*, 1989, and the *Nintendo Power* giveaway

Nintendo of America released *Dragon Warrior* in **August 1989** (the title changed because Simulations Publications held the *DragonQuest* tabletop trademark). The localization replaced passwords with battery saves, enlarged sprites, and rewrote dialogue in Elizabethan English. [10, 46] Sales were poor; Nintendo "vastly overestimated demand" and gave unsold copies away with *Nintendo Power* subscriptions. [10]

> [!NOTE]
> **The "500,000 copies given away" is a conflation.** The documented numbers are about 500,000 new subscribers from the free-copy offer and about 500,000 total US copies sold; no source gives a count of free cartridges. [15, 46]

Horii blamed the archaic translation; Torishima agreed it was the translation, not Toriyama's "cute" art. [1] The West then lost the series for most of a decade: *V* and *VI* went unreleased there, and acclaim arrived with *VII*, wider sales with *VIII*.

### 6.5 The Slime's footprint

The Slime appears in every mainline game, with a family that grew by accretion: King Slime, the slime stack from *IX*, and the Metal Slime as the series' experience-point lottery. The Dragon Quest Wiki lists 42 appearance subsections across the series, spin-offs, and crossovers. [43] Luida's Bar opened in Roppongi on **January 28, 2010**. [22] Dragon Quest Island on Horii's home island of Awaji has a King Slime photo spot. [38] The Hero joined *Super Smash Bros. Ultimate* on July 30, 2019. [23]

At the Tokyo Olympics opening ceremony on **July 23, 2021**, Sugiyama's "Overture" was the first piece played. [35, 42] The inclusion was contested: Sugiyama had a documented record of nationalist and historical-denial statements, and his obituaries in Game Developer and VGC called him a "nationalist figurehead" and "controversial." [34, 58]

### 6.6 Two deaths and Horii's constants

Sugiyama died on **September 30, 2021**, aged 90. [58] Toriyama died on **March 1, 2024**, aged 68, of an acute subdural hematoma, announced March 8. [33] Horii:

> "I am still in overwhelming disbelief at the news of Toriyama's sudden death. ... The history of *Dragon Quest* goes hand-in-hand with Toriyama's character designs."
> — Yuji Horii, March 2024 [33]

A year later: "He was very easy-going. He accepted the request of do-overs too." [32] Horii's design constants have not moved since 1986. In 2011: "My objective is to make the games intuitive and accessible for anybody"; "The main character in the game is actually the alter-ego of the player"; "At the end, you will always be rewarded for your hard work." [11] The constants are enforced: *IX*'s action-combat prototype was abandoned after fan criticism and turn-based combat restored. [51]

### 7.2 Kurita's 12×12 grid and the manga vocabulary

Kurita, born May 9, 1972, designed the 176 i-mode glyphs for a mobile internet whose emails were limited to 250 characters. Each glyph was **12×12 = 144 pixels, 18 bytes; the full set just over 3 KB**. [74, 83] He disliked the grid for a reason only a designer would notice: "the number of spaces in the grid was not an odd number," so no pixel sits at the center and true symmetry is impossible. [74] The vocabulary was manga's: MoMA says "Kurita drew on manga, the Zapf Dingbats typeface, and emoticons," and his listed influences include manga's symbolic marks such as a water drop for nervousness. [73, 83] He denied one source explicitly:

> "In creating emoji, I found inspiration in pictograms, manga, and all sorts of other sources. ... Both emoji and kanji are ideograms, but I did not find inspiration for designing emoji in the kanji."
> — Shigetaka Kurita [74]

Other carriers copied him: "every single one just took what we had and implemented it the way it was." [71] His later view is skeptical: "Contemporary emoji aren't really emoji... the majority of them are simply pictures." A pictogram that fits 144 pixels is a character; a rendered illustration is a picture. [74] MoMA still catalogues "Shigetaka Kurita. Emoji. 1998-1999," defensibly: his is the set with a named designer and the one the industry copied. [73]

### 7.3 💩 From Unchi-kun to U+1F4A9

*Dr. Slump*'s Unchi-kun is "one of several coiled feces that look much like soft ice cream," with "a sense of justice." [81, 85] The chain from that character to the code point has three links of very different strength.

**Documented.** The 1997 J-Phone poop was black-and-white with a smile and steam lines; Kurita wanted one and DoCoMo said "no good." [74, 84] At Google, headquarters found the animated poop offensive; the team countered with usage data showing it among the most popular emoji in Japan. Takeshi Kishimoto told Fast Company that the character gained popularity through the 1980s *Dr. Slump* anime, where it was "never serious." [75] Germick called the Gmail poop his "proudest achievement" and described its everyday use as "the anti-like." [75, 76]

**Cultural priming.** Wikipedia states that feces "had first taken on humorous connotations" in early-1980s Japan after *Dr. Slump*; the first sentence is uncited and names no designer. [84] Japanese culture supplied priming independent of Toriyama: *unko* begins with the same sound as the word for luck, and a Kyoto company began selling golden Kin no Unko charms around 2000, though that boom postdates the 1997 glyph and cannot have inspired it. [79, 84]

> [!WARNING]
> **Myth: "Akira Toriyama invented the emoji."** This is amplification with no primary evidence. No Toriyama statement about emoji exists; no SoftBank designer has been identified; no Apple designer has said the 2008 glyph came from Arale's toy. What can be said is that Toriyama's smiling soft-serve swirl normalized a cute anthropomorphic poop in Japan a decade before carrier emoji, and that the one Google employee on record explaining why it mattered pointed at *Dr. Slump*. That is cultural authorship of a visual idiom, which is real and is not the same as designing a glyph. [71, 75, 80]

The idiom's reach shows in the aftermath: a 2017 frowning-poo proposal failed, and Adobe's 2022 survey found the pile of poo the least-liked emoji in the United States even as it ranked among the most used. [77, 84]

### 7.4 Apple's role, and the absence of Jobs

Apple shipped its first emoji font on **November 21, 2008**, built to work with SoftBank, its Japanese launch partner; the keyboard was limited to Japan. That is why the modern Apple and Unicode poop descends from the 1997 J-Phone glyph rather than from DoCoMo, which never had one. [72, 82] Apple's set grew from 471 glyphs in 2008 to 2,776 in 2018. [72]

Jobs was CEO until August 24, 2011, so every decision through iOS 5 was made on his watch. But no source records any statement by Jobs about emoji, and no source documents SoftBank's role in negotiating Apple's. The "Jobs era" is chronological, not evidentiary. The iPhone carried the idiom; the idiom predated the product. [72]

### 8.1 Reed: Reynolds founded it, Palladino taught it

The calligraphy class was the product of a forty-year scholarly project. **Lloyd J. Reynolds** taught at Reed from 1929 to 1969 and launched a yearlong credit course in "alphabetic communication" in 1948-49, adopting the Italic hand specifically because it resembles printed type. [90] His doctrine reads like an interface-design manifesto:

> "A letter is a letter, not a picture of anything. It is not a picture of rhythmic movement; it *is* rhythmic movement."
> — Lloyd J. Reynolds [91]

Reynolds retired in 1969, three years before Jobs enrolled. The man at the front of the room was **Robert Palladino** (1932-2016), a former Trappist monk who left the order in 1968, studied with Reynolds, and took over the elective in fall 1969. [89, 90]

> [!NOTE]
> **Reed's own publications disagree.** Its main Jobs page credits Reynolds, while Reed Magazine's 2011 Jobs obituary and 2016 Palladino obituary name Palladino. Smithsonian Magazine ran a correction on October 7, 2011, settling on Reynolds as founder and Palladino as Jobs's teacher. Chronology resolves it. Palladino, who never used a computer, later said of Jobs: "He introduced me to the mouse." [87, 88, 97, 100]

Jobs told the story at Stanford on June 12, 2005: he learned serif and sans-serif, "about varying the amount of space between different letter combinations, about what makes great typography great," and ten years later, designing the first Macintosh, "it all came back." Without the course, the Mac "would have never had multiple typefaces or proportionally spaced fonts." [86]

### 8.2 Kare's city fonts and the LaserWriter

Susan Kare joined the Macintosh group as bitmap graphic designer. Her first font was a bold system face with no jagged diagonals; she and Andy Hertzfeld named the rest after stops on the Paoli Local. Then Jobs walked in:

> "What are those names?" ... "cities are OK, but not little cities that nobody's ever heard of. They ought to be WORLD CLASS cities!"
> — Steve Jobs, August 1983, as recounted by Susan Kare [96]

The fonts became Chicago, New York, Geneva, London, San Francisco, Toronto, and Venice. [96, 111] The city-naming is the only typographic decision on the Mac with a first-person witness attributing it to Jobs; the drawing was Kare's and Atkinson's. The convention outlived its author: Apple's system face since 2015 is San Francisco. [115] The most consequential typographic decision Jobs made personally was a deal: in December 1983 he convinced John Warnock to license PostScript; the LaserWriter was announced January 23, 1985 at $6,995. [114]

### 8.3 Markkula, Aspen, Esslinger, Rand, Rams, Ive

Jobs's design vocabulary was assembled from named sources. From Markkula's 1977 philosophy came three words: **Empathy** (understanding customer needs), **Focus** (eliminating distractions), and **Impute** (giving presentation the same rigor as the product). Jobs was still citing them decades later: "Mike taught me that." [93] At Aspen in 1983 he said computers could become "one more piece-of-junk-object," that making a product look great costs no more than making it look bad, and that computers and society were "out on a first date." He rejected Sony's "high-tech" surface ("It's easy to do that. But it's not great") in favor of packaging "just like Braun does with its electronics." [92, 98] Hartmut Esslinger won the contest to be Apple's Dieter Rams with "form follows emotion," and his language governed Apple products from 1984 to 1990. [98, 116] Paul Rand designed the NeXT logo for a flat $100,000. [107, 116]

The statements of principle cluster around one idea.

> "Most people make the mistake of thinking design is what it looks like... That's not what we think design is. It's not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works."
> — Steve Jobs, *NYT Magazine*, Nov 30, 2003 [110]

BusinessWeek, 1998: "Simple can be harder than complex: You have to work hard to get your thinking clean to make it simple." [108]

### 8.4 Zen, Hasui, and the Goyō print on the first Mac screen

Jobs's Japan ran through two channels. The personal one was Zen: Kōbun Chino Otogawa was his teacher, presided at his 1991 wedding, and answered Jobs's claim of enlightenment with a demand for "proof." Kōbun drowned in Switzerland on July 26, 2002. [102, 103, 113] The industrial channel was Sony: Akio Morita gave him a Walkman, and through Morita he met Issey Miyake, who made him about a hundred black turtlenecks. [92, 103, 106] Yet his Aspen verdict on Sony's styling was "easy... but not great." Japan gave Jobs a discipline and a benchmark, not a style; the claimed link between *ma* (negative space) and Apple's white space appears in no Jobs quotation. [92]

The print story ties typography to Japan directly. In March 1983 Jobs, Wozniak, and Rod Holt visited the Watanabe gallery in Ginza, and Jobs began collecting Kawase Hasui's shin-hanga. [105] At the January 24, 1984 launch, one of the first images on the Macintosh screen was Hashiguchi Goyō's 1920 print *Woman Combing Her Hair*, digitized by Kare from a scan by Bill Atkinson of a print that belonged to Jobs. [99, 104] A woodblock print, the medium that taught Japan to think in flat color and bounding line, was the demo graphic for the first mass-market bitmap display.

### 9.1 Flyers, rejections, and the Torishima machine

Toriyama's self-description begins with the fact that he did not train as a cartoonist. In 1980 he said "I came from a design course in high school," worked at a design company about three years, and disliked it: "I was forced to do things I'm not good with, like lettering." [117] In 2013 he gave the verdict that matters: "I hated it at the time, but having that experience behind me, I think it might have come in handy." [122] He left because he got home at 2 or 3 a.m., was late so often that three late arrivals counted as an absence, and was underpaid: "I thought this wouldn't do, so I decided to quit my job." No primary source says he was fired. [122]

Torishima found the Star Wars parody "beautiful and funny," then rejected about **500 pages**. Toriyama gave the figure in 1986, placing it before his 1978 debut; Torishima gave the same figure in 2016 but placed it in the eighteen months before *Dr. Slump*. [118, 134] Toriyama's motive was "almost entirely out of stubbornness." [122] Torishima made Arale the lead, told him a first Mashirito draft had "no unique traits. Make him even worse," and, when early *Dragon Ball* underperformed, said the hero was "rather plain," which produced the tournament format. [119, 123, 134] His doctrine: editors act as "director," "manager," and "producer"; the reader survey reflects only one moment, so the editor must form hypotheses "half step into the future"; and when readers respond strongly the editor-in-chief's view is "basically irrelevant." [134]

### 9.2 The "laziness" method

The "laziness" narrative is Toriyama's own framing, and its documentation is extensive. On the Super Saiyan's uninked hair: "That's because the first person to finish inking would have to be the one to erase all the pencil marks"; Torishima: "it's cutting corners, but I suppose the readers won't notice." [123, 125] The July 1995 *Daizenshuu 4* interview turns anecdotes into doctrine:

> "Basically with everything, I choose my criteria based on what can be easy."
> — Akira Toriyama, *Daizenshuu 4* (July 24, 1995) [120]

He built a fictional world because "if I made the real world the setting, I'd have to draw looking at reference materials"; he gave characters flight because planes and automobiles were "really tiresome," after which "the story's progress became speedy"; and he did not plot ahead "because it was a pain." [119, 120, 122] Read as engineering, every shortcut removes a recurring cost from a weekly pipeline of about fourteen pages while preserving legibility. A counter-rule governs when not to simplify: "If you draw it on the same level as the previous fight, it'll seem to the readers that the level has actually gone down." [121, 123] The popular one-liners ("I destroyed Namek because it was a pain") do not appear verbatim in any translated interview.

### 9.3 Cinema, machines, and games

His film diet is documented and almost entirely non-Japanese. *Drunken Master*: "If I hadn't seen this movie, I would never have come up with *Dragon Ball*." From 1980: *Star Wars*, *Alien* (later his favorite), *Enter the Dragon*, *Jaws*, and Frank Frazetta; original *Ultraman* because "I prefer things nice and simple." [117, 121] He plays stories "in his head as movie scenes." [121]

The one place he refused to simplify was machinery: "I also like building plastic models, the military kind"; "Nothing is wasted in the designs." *Dragon Ball* title pages carried real vehicles, and [117, 127, 128] Beyond *Dragon Quest*, his games work included *Chrono Trigger* (Square's "Dream Team" with Sakaguchi and Horii). [126, 132] Of himself: "The truth is, I didn't like being a manga artist very much" (2010). Eiichiro Oda's tribute in March 2024: "His existence is like a big tree." [131, 133]

### 10.1 Framing and pacing

All three learned from cinema that the audience should never wait. Jobs bought Pixar in 1986 for $10 million, carried it to *Toy Story*, and sold it to Disney on January 24, 2006 for $7.4 billion; he launched the Macintosh with Ridley Scott's "1984" commercial. [101, 112] Toriyama's most consequential pacing decision was giving characters flight, a cut to the world that eliminates travel scenes the way an editor cuts them. [120] Miyamoto's framing is spatial: the cave he hesitated to enter became the one affordance on *Zelda*'s first screen, and his objection to cutscenes that "make you watch rather than act" is a filmmaker's objection to exposition. [60, 62] The common move is to let the frame carry the meaning and keep exposition to one line.

### 10.2 Radical simplicity

Jobs moralized simplicity, from the 1977 brochure to "focus and simplicity" in 1998. [98, 108] Toriyama apologized for it ("what can be easy"), though his taste ("I prefer things nice and simple") preceded the deadline. [117, 120] Miyamoto engineered it: a mustache because a mouth and nose cannot be distinguished at 16 pixels, a hat because hair cannot be drawn, overalls because three frames need two colors to show motion. [61] Horii's version is accessibility: a 50-page scenario, yes/no as the only speech, and a Slime whose pixel-art first draft was rejected as lacking flavor, the one case here where simplicity was pushed back as too simple. [1, 4] The shared rule is that simplification must reduce noise, never signal: Jobs's "hard work to get your thinking clean," Toriyama's warning about the previous fight's level, Miyamoto's 20 percent difficulty reduction, and Horii's 7-XP level-up are all calibrations of how much to remove before the user stops feeling the thing.

### 10.3 Cross-disciplinary depth

Nothing in any of the three signatures came from inside its medium. Jobs's typography came from a former Trappist monk's Italic class, his industrial standard from Braun and the Bauhaus, his graphic identity from Paul Rand, and the first graphic on his flagship was a 1920 woodblock print. [89, 98, 104] Toriyama's line came from poster lettering and his staging from Jackie Chan; Miyamoto's legibility came from an unfulfilled manga career and his spaces from a Sonobe childhood. [68, 117, 123] *Dragon Quest* itself was an act of import: "the best of both" *Wizardry* and *Ultima*, filtered through a detective game's menus and scored by a man who answered a shogi questionnaire. Jobs articulated the mechanism ("Creativity is just connecting things"); Toriyama demonstrated it, saying only that the hated design job "might have come in handy." [109, 122] The adjacent discipline supplies the standard, not the content.

### 10.4 Constraint and editorial systems

Each worked inside a system that said no with a number attached. At Apple it was Markkula's three words and Jobs's own vetoes (no fan, no Sony surface, no "little cities that nobody's ever heard of"), with a one-bit display as the physical ceiling. [93, 96] At *Jump* it was the weekly reader survey, interpreted by an editor who rejected 500 pages; the children who mailed in cards held the veto. [134] At Nintendo it was the Famicom's silicon; at Enix, 64 KB plus a cartridge economy in which unsold ROMs were a total loss. [1, 69] Torishima's "half step into the future" is the manga version of Markkula's Empathy: a structured way of knowing what the user wants before the user does. The systems differ in who holds the veto (Jobs by temperament, the survey by design), and both produced global hits, which suggests the constant is not the source of the constraint but its severity.

### 10.5 Tutorialization

Three first screens from 1984-86 encode three theories of teaching. The Macintosh imputed taste: a Goyō print, world-city fonts, and files as icons on a desktop, in service of bringing "some liberal arts" to a machine society was meeting on "a first date." [92, 104] *Zelda* taught by withholding: one cave, one old man, one line, and a sealed map. [60] *Dragon Quest* taught by gating: a locked door, two mandatory commands, a level-up at 7 XP, preceded by a fifteen-week magazine explainer and a text adventure released the year before. [4, 39] All three refused the pop-up. The lesson was built into the first object the user saw, and the first object was designed as carefully as the last.

### 10.6 Economy of means

The numbers are tiny on purpose. Kurita's emoji: 12×12 pixels, 18 bytes. *Dragon Quest*: 64 KB including music, a 50-page scenario, a 30-level cap. Mario: 16×16 pixels, three frames. *Zelda*: 256 tiles, katakana only. The Mac: bitmap fonts without jagged diagonals. The Slime: two dots and a curve inside a teardrop. [1, 61, 67, 74, 96] In each case the designer treated the constraint as the specification, and the asset proved more durable than richer alternatives because it contained nothing that could date. Kurita's complaint that modern emoji are "simply pictures" and Alan Dye's admission that Apple "really had to rerender" its emoji to display them larger are the same observation from opposite sides. [72, 74] The Slime has needed no redesign since 1986; Mario's silhouette none since 1981.

### 10.7 Iconic abstraction

Scott McCloud's *Understanding Comics* (1993) supplies the canonical explanation: simply drawn "iconic" characters let readers project themselves into the story, and a minimal face leaves room for the reader's feeling. [135] Shigesato Itoi made the point about Toriyama in the 2013 exhibition catalogue: "a circle with a dot in the middle stands for an eye in manga." [124] Neil Cohn's work refines the claim: he and Sean Ehly catalogued 73 conventionalized graphic schemas and argued they form a "visual vocabulary" within a "Japanese Visual Language"; his EEG experiments showed scrambled panel sequences trigger the same response as ungrammatical sentences. [136, 137] So the readability of Toriyama's pages is partly fluency in a vocabulary *Jump* readers had already learned, and McCloud's universality claim has drawn criticism on that ground. [135]

The reconciliation is that the layers operate at different scales. The vocabulary (a sweat drop means nervousness; a menu says "Talk") is learned and local. The face (two dots and a curve; a mustache under a hat) is pre-linguistic, which is why the Slime, Mario, and the pile of poo need no instruction. All three designers kept the learned layer conventional (Geneva is "a redesigned Helvetica"; Goku's story is *Journey to the West*; *Dragon Quest* is *Wizardry* plus *Ultima*) and spent their originality on silhouette, contour, and one gesture. [115, 119] Jennequin's reversal test, Toriyama's "even when they're small," Kare's diagonal-free Chicago, Miyamoto's two-color overalls, and Kurita's 144 pixels are five ways of enforcing one requirement: the identity must survive the worst rendering it will ever face.

## 11. Principles for builders

| # | Rule | Evidence | Do this |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Design for the smallest rendering first** | Toriyama's "even when they're small"; Mario's pixel-saving features; Kurita's 144 pixels; Apple rerendering emoji in 2016 [16, 61, 72] | If the identity does not survive at 16 pixels or ten words, it is not yet an identity |
| 2 | **Treat the constraint as the specification** | 64 KB produced the single hero, tanka password, and locked throne room; 256 tiles produced katakana-only *Zelda* [1, 67] | When a team asks for more memory or time first, reduce the design further |
| 3 | **Ship the tutorial before the product** | *Portopia* on Famicom in 1985, then a fifteen-week *Jump* explainer [1, 39] | Teach a new category's grammar before its flagship is judged |
| 4 | **Make the first screen the lesson** | Locked throne room; one cave; a woodblock print on the Mac [4, 64, 104] | Build instruction into the first object the user sees, not a pop-up |
| 5 | **Decide who holds the leash, then commit** | Miyamoto sealed the map; Horii gated the exit; both won *Famitsu* awards [46, 60] | Do not hand over freedom and then interrupt with guidance |
| 6 | **Steal your standard from an adjacent discipline** | Calligraphy for Jobs, flyers for Toriyama, industrial design for Miyamoto [68, 86, 123] | Import a quality bar that insiders have stopped seeing |
| 7 | **Install an editor with a veto and a number** | Torishima's survey and 500 rejections; Markkula's three words; the rejected pixel-art Slime [1, 93, 134] | Use the number to kill what fails and a forward hypothesis to choose what next |
| 8 | **Reward effort within minutes** | The 7-XP level-up and red HP window [4] | The first visible reward sets the user's model of the whole product |
| 9 | **Economize where users will not notice; escalate where they will** | "What can be easy" paired with the previous-fight rule; the deleted iMac fan paired with packaging that sets "the tone" [93, 95, 120] | Reinvest what simplification saves in the moments that carry the experience |
| 10 | **Impute everything the user touches, including names** | "World class cities"; monsters named after the drawings [1, 96] | Treat names and first impressions as grammar |
| 11 | **Keep the mascot stable while the cast changes** | The Slime's "constant presence"; Chicago from Mac to iPod; *XII* with a dead artist's designs [29, 53, 115] | Do not redesign the element that accumulates meaning |
| 12 | **Own the idiom, not just the artifact** | Google reached for *Dr. Slump* to justify the poop; Sakaguchi needed *Dragon Quest*'s password save to believe in a Famicom RPG [36, 75] | The durable asset is the language others will reimplement |

## 13. Conclusion

The conventional stories are that taste made Apple, laziness made *Dragon Ball*, and genius made Nintendo. The record supports none of them. What made the Macintosh, the Slime, Mario, and *Zelda*'s first screen was one procedure run four times in about two years: a quality bar imported from a discipline next door, an external judge with a number attached, and a resource ceiling low enough that only the identity-bearing marks survived. "Taste," "what can be easy," and "form dictated by function" are three men's names for one method: remove until it breaks, then put back one thing. Miyamoto's sealed map and Horii's locked door are not rival answers to how much help a product should give; they show that the commitment matters more than the answer, and that the first screen is where it is made. The series that passed 100 million copies on September 16, 2026 will ship its next game with the drawings of an artist who never wanted the job and the music of a composer who answered a shogi questionnaire, because Horii understood in 1986 what the comparison makes explicit: the asset that survives is the one designed for the worst screen it will ever face.
